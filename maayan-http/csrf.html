CSRF — a request you never made, sent in your name

Cross-Site Request Forgery. The browser attaches your cookies to every request to a site — even a request triggered by a different, malicious site. So an attacker's page can make your browser act as the logged-in you.

The one idea

Cookies are automatic. If you're logged into bank.com, your browser sends the bank's session cookie on any request to bank.com — no matter who caused that request. The bank sees a valid cookie and assumes you meant it.

Attack shape: you're logged into the bank in one tab. In another tab you open evil.com. Its HTML quietly fires a request at the bank. Your browser attaches the bank cookie. The transfer goes through.

Example 1 — an image tag that moves money

If the action is a GET, even an <img> on the attacker's page triggers it. The browser fetches the "image" and sends the cookie:

<!-- sitting on evil.com --> <img src="https://bank.com/transfer?to=attacker&amount=5000">

The browser turns that into a real request with your bank cookie attached. No click needed.

Example 2 — an auto-submitting form (POST)

POST doesn't save you. A hidden form submits itself with JavaScript:

<!-- evil.com --> <form action="https://bank.com/transfer" method="POST"> <input type=hidden name="to" value="attacker"> <input type=hidden name="amount" value="5000"> </form> <script>document.forms[0].submit()</script>

What the bank actually receives — a normal-looking POST, cookie and all:

POST /transfer HTTP/1.1 Host: bank.com Cookie: session=abc123… ← attached automatically Origin: https://evil.com ← the tell: request came from elsewhere to=attacker&amount=5000

The bank can't tell this from a real one — the cookie is genuine. The only clue is that Origin isn't the bank.

Why it works — the rationale

  • Cookies identify you by destination, not by who initiated the request.
  • A page can send requests to other origins (images, forms) — it just can't read the response (Same-Origin Policy). For a state-changing action, sending is enough.
  • So authentication alone proves you're logged in — not that you intended this request.

The fix

1. CSRF token. Server plants a secret random value in the page (a form field / header) that the attacker's site can't read or guess. Real requests carry it; forged ones don't.

POST /transfer HTTP/1.1 Cookie: session=abc123… X-CSRF-Token: 9f3a… ← evil.com can't know this → request rejected to=friend&amount=50

Cookie proves who you are; the token proves the request came from the bank's own page.

2. SameSite cookies. Tell the browser not to attach the cookie on cross-site requests. One line, kills most CSRF:

Set-Cookie: session=abc123; SameSite=Lax; Secure; HttpOnly

Lax is the modern browser default. Strict is tighter. This is the laziest real defense.

3. Check Origin. Reject state-changing requests whose Origin header isn't your own site.

Rule of thumb: GET must never change state. Anything that does (POST/PUT/DELETE) needs a CSRF token or SameSite cookies. Use both.

CSRF exploits the same thing that makes login convenient: the browser sends your cookie automatically. The defense is proving intent, not just identity. See also HTTP/1.1.
